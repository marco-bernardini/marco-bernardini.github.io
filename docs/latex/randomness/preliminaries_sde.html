Probability Preliminaries

Before studying stochastic processes, it is useful to understand the basic objects on which probability theory is built.

The definitions can initially look unnecessarily abstract. They are not.

The main purpose of this section is to understand this construction and become familiar with the language that will be used later.

From outcomes to events

Sample space

A random experiment may have many possible outcomes. We collect all of them in a set called the sample space

\[ \Omega. \]

An individual outcome is denoted by \(\omega\in\Omega\).

For a coin toss,

\[ \Omega=\{H,T\}. \]

For a stochastic trajectory, however, an outcome \(\omega\) may eventually represent an entire path. Thus \(\Omega\) should be thought of abstractly as the universe containing all possible realizations of the random system.

Usually we are not interested in individual outcomes, but in statements about them. Such statements correspond to subsets of \(\Omega\).

For example,

\[ A=\{\omega:\text{the outcome satisfies some property}\} \]

is called an event.

We would like to assign probabilities to events.

The first temptation would therefore be to assign probabilities to every subset of \(\Omega\). For finite spaces this works perfectly well. For sufficiently complicated infinite spaces, however, it is impossible to construct a useful probability measure on every possible subset.

We therefore specify which subsets are admissible events.

Fields and \(\sigma\)-algebras

Suppose \(A\) is an event. Then we naturally also want

\[ A^c \]

to be an event.

Likewise, if \(A\) and \(B\) are events, then statements such as

\[ A\cup B \]

and

\[ A\cap B \]

should also be events.

This motivates the algebraic structure imposed on collections of events.

A collection \(\mathcal{F}\) of subsets of \(\Omega\) is called a field if

\[ \emptyset\in\mathcal{F}, \]

\[ A\in\mathcal{F} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A^c\in\mathcal{F}, \]

and

\[ A,B\in\mathcal{F} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A\cup B\in\mathcal{F}. \]

Consequently, a field is closed under finite unions and finite intersections.

In probability we regularly encounter limits involving infinitely many events. Finite closure is therefore not enough.

A \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{F}\) strengthens the previous requirement by demanding closure under countable unions:

\[ A_1,A_2,\ldots\in\mathcal{F} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\in\mathcal{F}. \]

Together with closure under complements, this also implies

\[ \bigcap_{n=1}^\infty A_n\in\mathcal{F}. \]

So a \(\sigma\)-algebra is simply a collection of events stable under the logical operations that probability theory needs.

Generated \(\sigma\)-algebras

Often we begin with a natural collection of sets \(\mathcal{C}\) but it is not itself a \(\sigma\)-algebra.

We then define

\[ \sigma(\mathcal{C}) \]

to be the smallest \(\sigma\)-algebra containing \(\mathcal{C}\).

This is an important recurring construction: rather than arbitrarily choosing a huge collection of measurable sets, we start from the sets that matter and add exactly what is required for the \(\sigma\)-algebra axioms to hold.

The Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra

Suppose our state space is \(\mathbb{R}\).

Intervals such as

\[ (a,b), \qquad (-\infty,x], \]

are clearly sets to which we would like to assign probabilities.

Instead of choosing all subsets of \(\mathbb{R}\), we begin with its open sets and generate a \(\sigma\)-algebra from them:

\[ \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}) = \sigma(\text{open subsets of }\mathbb{R}). \]

This is called the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra.

More generally,

\[ \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d) \]

is the \(\sigma\)-algebra generated by the open subsets of \(\mathbb{R}^d\).

The Borel sets are therefore the sets naturally compatible with the topology of the state space.

Measurable and probability spaces

A set together with a \(\sigma\)-algebra,

\[ (\Omega,\mathcal{F}), \]

is called a measurable space.

At this stage there is still no probability. We have only specified which questions about \(\omega\) we are allowed to ask.

We now attach probabilities to those events.

A probability measure is a function

\[ \mathbb{P}:\mathcal{F}\to[0,1] \]

such that

\[ \mathbb{P}(\Omega)=1, \qquad \mathbb{P}(\emptyset)=0, \]

and for pairwise disjoint events \(A_1,A_2,\ldots\),

\[ \mathbb{P} \left( \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n \right) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mathbb{P}(A_n). \]

The triple

\[ (\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}) \]

is called a probability space.

The three objects play distinct roles:

  • \(\Omega\) describes what can happen;
  • \(\mathcal{F}\) describes which events we can distinguish;
  • \(\mathbb{P}\) assigns probabilities to those events.

Random variables

A random variable is not itself a random outcome. It is a function applied to the outcome.

Let

\[ (\Omega,\mathcal{F}) \]

be our underlying measurable space and let \((E,\mathcal{G})\) be another measurable space.

A random variable is a measurable function

\[ X:\Omega\to E. \]

The word measurable means that

\[ X^{-1}(A)\in\mathcal{F} \]

for every \(A\in\mathcal{G}\).

Why is this condition necessary?

Suppose we want to ask

\[ \mathbb{P}(X\in A). \]

The probability measure \(\mathbb{P}\) acts on subsets of \(\Omega\), not on subsets of \(E\).

But

\[ X\in A \]

really means

\[ \{\omega\in\Omega:X(\omega)\in A\} = X^{-1}(A). \]

Hence

\[ \mathbb{P}(X\in A) = \mathbb{P}\left(X^{-1}(A)\right). \]

For this expression to make sense, \(X^{-1}(A)\) must belong to \(\mathcal{F}\).

That is precisely what measurability guarantees.

For a real-valued random variable,

\[ X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}, \]

we normally take \(\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\).

The law of a random variable

The underlying probability space may be extremely complicated. Often we do not actually care about its detailed structure.

Once a random variable

\[ X:\Omega\to E \]

has been defined, it transfers the probability measure \(\mathbb{P}\) from \(\Omega\) to \(E\).

Define

\[ \mu_X(A) = \mathbb{P}(X\in A) = \mathbb{P}(X^{-1}(A)). \]

The measure \(\mu_X\) is called the law or distribution of \(X\).

Schematically,

\[ (\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}) \xrightarrow{\quad X\quad} (E,\mathcal{G},\mu_X). \]

This induced measure is also called the pushforward measure of \(\mathbb{P}\) through \(X\):

\[ \mu_X=X_\#\mathbb{P}. \]

This distinction is useful:

  • \(\mathbb{P}\) describes randomness on the underlying sample space;
  • \(\mu_X\) describes the resulting distribution of the observable \(X\).

Two completely different probability spaces can therefore produce random variables with exactly the same law.

For \(X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\), the cumulative distribution function is

\[ F_X(x) = \mathbb{P}(X\leq x). \]

Expectation

Expectation is simply integration with respect to probability.

For an integrable random variable \(X\),

\[ \mathbb{E}[X] = \int_\Omega X(\omega)\,d\mathbb{P}(\omega). \]

But since \(X\) induces its own probability measure \(\mu_X\), the same quantity can be computed directly on the state space:

\[ \mathbb{E}[X] = \int_E x\,\mu_X(dx). \]

More generally,

\[ \mathbb{E}[f(X)] = \int_\Omega f(X(\omega))\,d\mathbb{P}(\omega) = \int_E f(x)\,\mu_X(dx). \]

If \(X\) admits a probability density \(p_X\), then

\[ \mathbb{E}[f(X)] = \int_E f(x)p_X(x)\,dx. \]

This is useful conceptually: once the law of \(X\) is known, expectations involving only \(X\) can be computed without referring explicitly to the original sample space \(\Omega\).

\(L^p\) spaces

A random variable is also a measurable function, so we can classify random variables according to their integrability.

For \(1\leq p<\infty\), define

\[ L^p(\Omega) = \left\{ X: \mathbb{E}[|X|^p]<\infty \right\}. \]

The associated norm is

\[ \|X\|_{L^p} = \left( \mathbb{E}[|X|^p] \right)^{1/p}. \]

Thus

\[ X\in L^1 \]

means that \(X\) has a finite absolute expectation, while

\[ X\in L^2 \]

means that \(X\) has a finite second moment.

The space \(L^2\) is particularly important because it is a Hilbert space with inner product

\[ \langle X,Y\rangle_{L^2} = \mathbb{E}[XY]. \]

This geometric interpretation will appear repeatedly later.

As usual in measure theory, two random variables that differ only on a set of probability zero are identified as the same element of \(L^p\).

This is why statements in probability are so often made almost surely.

A property holds almost surely if the set on which it fails has probability zero.

Covariance

Expectation describes the average behaviour of one variable. Covariance describes how two variables move together.

For \(X,Y\in L^2\),

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = \mathbb{E} \left[ (X-\mathbb{E}[X]) (Y-\mathbb{E}[Y]) \right]. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]. \]

The variance is

\[ \operatorname{Var}(X) = \operatorname{Cov}(X,X). \]

The normalized version is the correlation coefficient

\[ \rho(X,Y) = \frac{ \operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) }{ \sqrt{ \operatorname{Var}(X) \operatorname{Var}(Y) } }. \]

If

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=0, \]

we say that \(X\) and \(Y\) are uncorrelated.

This only says that there is no linear dependence detectable through covariance. It does not, in general, imply independence.

Independence

Two random variables are independent when knowing one gives no probabilistic information about the other.

Formally, \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent if

\[ \mathbb{P}(X\in A,Y\in B) = \mathbb{P}(X\in A) \mathbb{P}(Y\in B) \]

for every measurable \(A\) and \(B\).

Equivalently, their joint law factorizes:

\[ \mu_{X,Y} = \mu_X\otimes\mu_Y. \]

If densities exist,

\[ p_{X,Y}(x,y) = p_X(x)p_Y(y). \]

Independence implies, whenever the relevant expectations exist,

\[ \mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y], \]

and therefore

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=0. \]

The reverse implication is generally false.

The distinction is important:

\[ \text{independence} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{uncorrelated}, \]

but not conversely.

Conditional expectation

Suppose we do not know the entire outcome \(\omega\), but only some information about it.

Mathematically, information is represented by a sub-\(\sigma\)-algebra

\[ \mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}. \]

A smaller \(\sigma\)-algebra contains fewer distinguishable events and therefore represents less information.

If \(X\in L^1\), the conditional expectation

\[ \mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}] \]

is a \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable random variable satisfying

\[ \int_G \mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}] \,d\mathbb{P} = \int_G X\,d\mathbb{P} \]

for every \(G\in\mathcal{G}\).

The important point is that

\[ \mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}] \]

is itself a random variable.

It depends on \(\omega\), but it is allowed to depend only on the information contained in \(\mathcal{G}\).

In this sense, conditional expectation replaces \(X\) with the best summary of \(X\) that can be constructed using the available information.

For \(X\in L^2\), this interpretation becomes geometric: \(\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]\) is the orthogonal projection of \(X\) onto the subspace of \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable random variables in \(L^2\).

Basic properties

Conditional expectation is linear:

\[ \mathbb{E} [ aX+bY \mid\mathcal{G} ] = a\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}] + b\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{G}]. \]

If \(X\) is already \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable, then no information has been lost:

\[ \mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}] = X. \]

If \(X\) is independent of \(\mathcal{G}\), then \(\mathcal{G}\) tells us nothing about \(X\):

\[ \mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}] = \mathbb{E}[X]. \]

If \(Y\) is \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable, then

\[ \mathbb{E}[YX\mid\mathcal{G}] = Y\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]. \]

If

\[ \mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}, \]

then the tower property gives

\[ \mathbb{E} \left[ \mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}] \mid\mathcal{H} \right] = \mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{H}]. \]

In particular,

\[ \mathbb{E} \left[ \mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}] \right] = \mathbb{E}[X]. \]

Conditional probabilities are particular conditional expectations:

\[ \mathbb{P}(A\mid\mathcal{G}) = \mathbb{E} [ \mathbf{1}_A \mid\mathcal{G} ]. \]

Convergence of random variables

Sequences of random variables appear everywhere in probability and stochastic analysis.

Typically, the object we care about is too difficult to manipulate directly, so we construct approximations

\[ X_1,X_2,\ldots \]

and ask whether

\[ X_n\to X. \]

Unlike for deterministic numbers, there is more than one meaningful answer to this question.

The different notions of convergence describe different things that may be converging.

Almost sure convergence

We say that

\[ X_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}X \]

if

\[ \mathbb{P} \left( \lim_{n\to\infty}X_n=X \right) = 1. \]

Equivalently, for almost every \(\omega\),

\[ X_n(\omega)\to X(\omega). \]

This is therefore a pointwise statement about realizations.

Once \(\omega\) is fixed, we simply have an ordinary deterministic sequence

\[ X_1(\omega),X_2(\omega),\ldots \]

converging to \(X(\omega)\).

Convergence in probability

We say that

\[ X_n\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}X \]

if, for every \(\varepsilon>0\),

\[ \mathbb{P} \left( |X_n-X|>\varepsilon \right) \to0. \]

Here we no longer require convergence for almost every individual \(\omega\).

Instead, we require the probability of making an error larger than \(\varepsilon\) to vanish.

This is therefore a weaker notion than almost sure convergence:

\[ X_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}X \quad\Longrightarrow\quad X_n\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}X. \]

\(L^p\) convergence

We say that

\[ X_n\xrightarrow{L^p}X \]

if

\[ \mathbb{E} \left[ |X_n-X|^p \right] \to0. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \|X_n-X\|_{L^p}\to0. \]

This controls the size of the approximation error in an averaged moment sense.

For example,

\[ X_n\xrightarrow{L^2}X \]

means

\[ \mathbb{E} \left[ |X_n-X|^2 \right] \to0. \]

This is usually called mean-square convergence.

For \(p\geq1\),

\[ X_n\xrightarrow{L^p}X \quad\Longrightarrow\quad X_n\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}X. \]

Almost sure convergence and \(L^p\) convergence are generally not comparable without additional assumptions.

Convergence in distribution

Sometimes we do not care whether \(X_n(\omega)\) approaches \(X(\omega)\) for the same outcome \(\omega\).

We care only whether their probability distributions become indistinguishable.

Let \(F_n\) and \(F\) be the distribution functions of \(X_n\) and \(X\).

We say that

\[ X_n\xrightarrow{d}X \]

if

\[ F_n(x)\to F(x) \]

at every continuity point of \(F\).

This is called convergence in distribution.

It is weaker than convergence in probability:

\[ X_n\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}X \quad\Longrightarrow\quad X_n\xrightarrow{d}X. \]

Importantly, \(X_n\) and \(X\) do not even need to live on the same underlying probability space. Only their laws matter.

Weak convergence of probability measures

Convergence in distribution becomes particularly transparent when written in terms of probability measures.

Let

\[ \mu_n=\mathcal{L}(X_n), \qquad \mu=\mathcal{L}(X). \]

We say that \(\mu_n\) converges weakly to \(\mu\),

\[ \mu_n\Rightarrow\mu, \]

if

\[ \int_E f(x)\,\mu_n(dx) \longrightarrow \int_E f(x)\,\mu(dx) \]

for every bounded continuous function \(f\).

Equivalently,

\[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_n)] \longrightarrow \mathbb{E}[f(X)] \]

for every bounded continuous \(f\).

Thus

\[ X_n\xrightarrow{d}X \]

means exactly

\[ \mathcal{L}(X_n) \Rightarrow \mathcal{L}(X). \]

So convergence in distribution is weak convergence of the laws of the random variables.

This is conceptually different from the previous notions:

  • almost sure convergence compares individual realizations;
  • \(L^p\) convergence compares their average distance;
  • convergence in probability asks whether large errors become unlikely;
  • convergence in distribution compares only the induced probability measures.

The basic implication structure is

\[ X_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}X \quad\Longrightarrow\quad X_n\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}X \quad\Longrightarrow\quad X_n\xrightarrow{d}X, \]

and

\[ X_n\xrightarrow{L^p}X \quad\Longrightarrow\quad X_n\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}X. \]

The reverse implications do not hold in general.
