Martingales and Other Beasts

Before introducing Brownian motion, it is useful to organize the main objects that appear in stochastic analysis.

A stochastic process is not merely a collection of random variables. We also care about:

  • how information accumulates through time;
  • whether the process has memory;
  • whether its statistical properties change through time;
  • whether its future values are, in a precise sense, unpredictable from current information.

These ideas lead naturally to filtrations, martingales, stationary processes, Gaussian processes, and Markov processes.

Finite-dimensional distributions

A stochastic process contains an uncountable collection of random variables, but we can probe its law through finite collections of times.

For times

\[ t_1,\ldots,t_n, \]

consider the random vector

\[ (X_{t_1},\ldots,X_{t_n}). \]

Its distribution is called a finite-dimensional distribution of \(X\).

The collection of all such distributions describes the joint probabilistic behaviour of the process at arbitrary finite sets of times.

This is an important distinction:

\[ \text{law of one }X_t \]

describes the process at one time, whereas

\[ \text{finite-dimensional distributions} \]

also describe dependence across time.

Two processes can have identical one-time distributions while having completely different dynamics.

Stationary processes

A process is called stationary when its statistical behaviour does not change under shifts in time.

There are two important notions.

Strict stationarity

A stochastic process \(\{X_t\}\) is strictly stationary if, for every \(n\), every choice of times \(t_1,\ldots,t_n\), and every time shift \(h\),

\[ (X_{t_1},\ldots,X_{t_n}) \overset{d}{=} (X_{t_1+h},\ldots,X_{t_n+h}), \]

whenever the shifted times remain in the time domain.

Thus all finite-dimensional distributions are invariant under time translation.

Strict stationarity is therefore a statement about the entire law of the process.

Weak stationarity

Suppose \(X_t\in L^2\).

The process is weakly stationary, or second-order stationary, if

\[ \mathbb{E}[X_t] = \mu \]

is independent of \(t\), and the covariance depends only on the time difference:

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(X_t,X_s) = C(t-s). \]

For a centered process,

\[ \mathbb{E}[X_t]=0, \]

this becomes

\[ C(t-s) = \mathbb{E}[X_tX_s]. \]

The function \(C\) is called the autocovariance or correlation function.

Thus weak stationarity says that the first two moments are invariant under time translation.

Strict stationarity is stronger in general:

\[ \text{strict stationarity} + X_t\in L^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{weak stationarity}. \]

The converse is generally false.

Stationarity versus stationary increments

These concepts should not be confused.

A process has stationary increments if the law of

\[ X_{t+h}-X_t \]

depends only on \(h\), not on \(t\).

That is,

\[ X_{t+h}-X_t \overset{d}{=} X_h-X_0. \]

A process may have stationary increments without itself being stationary.

This distinction will matter immediately when we introduce Brownian motion.

Martingales

Martingales formalize the idea of a process with no predictable drift.

Let \(\{X_t\}\) be adapted to a filtration \(\{\mathcal{F}_t\}\).

The process is a martingale if

\[ \mathbb{E}[|X_t|]<\infty \]

for every \(t\), and for all \(s\leq t\),

\[ \mathbb{E}[X_t\mid\mathcal{F}_s] = X_s. \]

This is one of the central definitions of stochastic analysis.

The interpretation is:

given all information available at time \(s\), the best prediction of the future value \(X_t\) is simply the current value \(X_s\).

A martingale is therefore a mathematical model of a fair game.

A martingale does not mean independence

The condition

\[ \mathbb{E}[X_t\mid\mathcal{F}_s] = X_s \]

does not mean that \(X_t\) is independent of the past.

Indeed, \(X_t\) generally depends strongly on \(X_s\).

The condition concerns only its conditional mean.

Equivalently,

\[ \mathbb{E}[X_t-X_s\mid\mathcal{F}_s] = 0. \]

So the future increment has zero conditional expectation.

Constant expectation

Using the tower property,

\[ \mathbb{E}[X_t] = \mathbb{E} \left[ \mathbb{E}[X_t\mid\mathcal{F}_s] \right] = \mathbb{E}[X_s]. \]

Hence every martingale satisfies

\[ \mathbb{E}[X_t] = \mathbb{E}[X_0]. \]

A martingale therefore has constant mean.

The converse is false: a process can have constant expectation without being a martingale.

A martingale is a conditional statement, not merely an unconditional one.

Martingales from conditional expectations

Let \(Y\in L^1\) and define

\[ M_t = \mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{F}_t]. \]

Then \(\{M_t\}\) is a martingale.

Indeed, if \(s\leq t\),

\[ \mathbb{E}[M_t\mid\mathcal{F}_s] = \mathbb{E} \left[ \mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{F}_t] \mid\mathcal{F}_s \right] = \mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{F}_s] = M_s. \]

This is perhaps the cleanest interpretation of martingales:

\[ M_t = \text{our current best estimate of some future quantity }Y. \]

As information grows, the estimate changes, but it remains conditionally unbiased.

Submartingales and supermartingales

A process is a submartingale if

\[ \mathbb{E}[X_t\mid\mathcal{F}_s] \geq X_s, \qquad s\leq t, \]

and a supermartingale if

\[ \mathbb{E}[X_t\mid\mathcal{F}_s] \leq X_s. \]

Thus, informally,

\[ \text{submartingale} \quad\leftrightarrow\quad \text{non-negative expected drift}, \]

while

\[ \text{supermartingale} \quad\leftrightarrow\quad \text{non-positive expected drift}. \]

Martingales are the boundary case where the conditional expected drift is zero.

Convex transformations

If \(X_t\) is a martingale and \(\varphi\) is convex, then Jensen’s inequality for conditional expectations gives

\[ \varphi(X_t) \]

as a submartingale, provided the relevant expectations exist.

Indeed,

\[ \mathbb{E} [ \varphi(X_t) \mid \mathcal{F}_s ] \geq \varphi \left( \mathbb{E}[X_t\mid\mathcal{F}_s] \right) = \varphi(X_s). \]

For example, if \(X_t\) is a martingale,

\[ X_t^2 \]

is typically a submartingale.

Why martingales matter

Martingales will become central for at least three reasons.

First, stochastic integrals of the Itô type are naturally martingales.

Second, many stochastic processes can be decomposed into

\[ \text{predictable drift} + \text{martingale noise}. \]

Third, martingale arguments provide powerful tools for stopping problems, stochastic control, finance, and PDE representations.

Gaussian processes

Before defining a Gaussian process, recall that a random vector

\[ X=(X_1,\ldots,X_n) \]

is jointly Gaussian if every linear combination

\[ a_1X_1+\cdots+a_nX_n \]

is a Gaussian random variable.

A stochastic process \(\{X_t\}_{t\in T}\) is a Gaussian process if every finite-dimensional random vector

\[ (X_{t_1},\ldots,X_{t_n}) \]

is jointly Gaussian.

Thus, a Gaussian process is not defined by requiring each \(X_t\) individually to be Gaussian. The variables must be jointly Gaussian across time.

Mean and covariance determine a Gaussian process

Define the mean function

\[ m(t) = \mathbb{E}[X_t] \]

and covariance function

\[ C(t,s) = \operatorname{Cov}(X_t,X_s). \]

For any finite collection \(t_1,\ldots,t_n\),

\[ (X_{t_1},\ldots,X_{t_n}) \]

has mean vector

\[ \mu_i=m(t_i) \]

and covariance matrix

\[ K_{ij} = C(t_i,t_j). \]

Since a Gaussian random vector is completely determined by its mean and covariance matrix, a Gaussian process is completely characterized by

\[ m(t) \]

and

\[ C(t,s). \]

This is one reason Gaussian processes are unusually tractable: infinitely many finite-dimensional distributions are encoded by only two deterministic functions.

Valid covariance functions

A covariance function must satisfy

\[ C(t,s)=C(s,t) \]

and must be non-negative definite.

For arbitrary times \(t_1,\ldots,t_n\) and coefficients \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\),

\[ \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n a_i a_j C(t_i,t_j) \geq 0. \]

Indeed,

\[ \sum_{i,j} a_i a_j C(t_i,t_j) = \operatorname{Var} \left( \sum_i a_i X_{t_i} \right) \geq0. \]

Hence one cannot choose an arbitrary function \(C(t,s)\) and declare it to be a covariance kernel.

Gaussian processes and stationarity

For a Gaussian process, weak stationarity is enough to imply strict stationarity.

The reason is that the finite-dimensional distributions are completely determined by their means and covariance matrices.

Hence, if

\[ \mathbb{E}[X_t] = \mu \]

and

\[ C(t,s) = C(t-s), \]

then shifting all times leaves the mean vector and covariance matrix unchanged.

Therefore,

\[ \text{Gaussian + weakly stationary} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{strictly stationary}. \]

For Gaussian processes, the two notions of stationarity therefore coincide, assuming finite second moments.

Uncorrelated Gaussian variables are independent

Another special feature of Gaussian systems is that zero covariance implies independence.

If \(X\) and \(Y\) are jointly Gaussian and

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=0, \]

then

\[ X\perp Y. \]

This fact will later make Gaussian processes particularly easy to analyze through their covariance functions.

Markov processes

A Markov process formalizes a different idea: the future depends on the present, but not on the entire past.

Let

\[ \mathcal{F}_t^X = \sigma(X_s:s\leq t) \]

be the natural filtration of \(X\).

The process is Markov if, for every \(s\leq t\) and every measurable set \(A\),

\[ \mathbb{P} ( X_t\in A \mid \mathcal{F}_s^X ) = \mathbb{P} ( X_t\in A \mid X_s ). \]

The left-hand side conditions on the entire history

\[ \{X_r:r\leq s\}, \]

whereas the right-hand side conditions only on the current state \(X_s\).

Thus the Markov property says:

once the present state is known, the past contains no additional information useful for predicting the future.

Markov does not mean independent

A Markov process can have very strong temporal dependence.

The Markov property does not say

\[ X_t \perp X_s. \]

Rather, it says that the present state contains all relevant information from the past.

Schematically,

\[ \text{past} \longrightarrow X_s \longrightarrow \text{future}. \]

Once \(X_s\) is given, the earlier history becomes irrelevant for predicting the future.

Transition probabilities

A Markov process can be described through its transition probabilities

\[ P(A,t\mid x,s) = \mathbb{P} ( X_t\in A \mid X_s=x ). \]

If a density exists, we write

\[ P(A,t\mid x,s) = \int_A p(y,t\mid x,s)\,dy, \]

where

\[ p(y,t\mid x,s) \]

is the transition density.

It tells us the distribution of the future state \(X_t\) given that the process is at \(x\) at time \(s\).

Time-homogeneous Markov processes

A Markov process is time-homogeneous if its transition probabilities depend only on the elapsed time:

\[ P(A,t\mid x,s) = P(A,t-s\mid x,0). \]

We can therefore write

\[ P_\tau(x,A) = \mathbb{P} ( X_{t+\tau}\in A \mid X_t=x ). \]

For a time-homogeneous process, the transition law depends on

\[ \text{current state} + \text{time elapsed}, \]

but not on the absolute calendar time.

The Chapman–Kolmogorov equation

Suppose

\[ s<u<t. \]

A Markov process can travel from \(x\) at time \(s\) to a set \(A\) at time \(t\) by passing through some intermediate state \(y\) at time \(u\).

Summing over all possible intermediate states gives the Chapman–Kolmogorov equation

\[ P(A,t\mid x,s) = \int_E P(A,t\mid y,u) P(dy,u\mid x,s). \]

If transition densities exist,

\[ p(z,t\mid x,s) = \int_E p(z,t\mid y,u) p(y,u\mid x,s)\,dy. \]

This is the probabilistic analogue of a composition law.

For a time-homogeneous Markov process,

\[ P_{t+s} = P_tP_s. \]

This semigroup structure will later lead directly to generators and Kolmogorov equations.

Markovian state variables and modelling

Whether a model is Markov depends crucially on the choice of state variables.

A process may fail to be Markov because its current value does not contain enough information about its future evolution.

Sometimes this can be fixed by enlarging the state.

For example, suppose position satisfies

\[ X_t = X_0 + \int_0^t V_s\,ds. \]

Knowing \(X_t\) alone may not be sufficient to predict the future, because the current velocity \(V_t\) also matters.

But the enlarged state

\[ (X_t,V_t) \]

may be Markov.

This is an important modelling principle:

choose the state so that the present contains all information required to determine future probabilities.

Markovianity is therefore not merely a mathematical property. It is also a statement about whether we have chosen an adequate state representation.

How the different ideas relate

The properties introduced above describe different aspects of a stochastic process.

Stationarity concerns invariance through time:

\[ \text{do the statistics change when time is shifted?} \]

Gaussianity concerns the shape of finite-dimensional distributions:

\[ \text{are all finite collections jointly Gaussian?} \]

The Markov property concerns memory:

\[ \text{is the present state sufficient to predict the future?} \]

The martingale property concerns conditional drift:

\[ \text{is the current value already the best prediction of the future value?} \]

These properties are logically distinct.

A process can be Markov but not stationary.

A process can be stationary but not Markov.

A process can be Gaussian but not Markov.

A process can be Markov but not a martingale.

The interesting stochastic processes we encounter later arise from particular combinations of these properties.

Brownian motion, for example, will turn out to be a continuous Gaussian Markov process with stationary independent increments and a martingale structure.

At that point, rather than memorizing a list of mysterious Brownian-motion properties, we will already know what each of those words means.
